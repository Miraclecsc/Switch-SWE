As for your other questions: > Silly question to start: Why is it called "fractional peeling" and "fractional edge values"? Not silly at all. The FISTA-based algorithm works quite differently. I realized I forgot to add a docstring explaining how the algorithm actually works, so the latest commit should provide more context. In short: Harb et al. [1] showed that computing the densest subgraph can be formulated as the following convex optimization problem: Minimize $\sum_{u \in V(G)} b_u^2$ Subject to: $b_u = \sum_{v : (u,v) \in E(G)} x_{uv}~$ for all $u \in V(G)$ $x_{uv} + x_{vu} = 1.0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~$ for all $(u,v) \in E(G)$ $x_{uv}\geq 0, x_{vu}\geq 0~~~~~~~~~~~~~$ for all $(u,v) \in E(G)$ Here, $x_{uv}$ represents the fraction of edge $(u,v)$ assigned to $u$, and $x_{vu}$ to $v$. So each edge distributes its weight among its two neighbors. The **optimal solution** of this convex program provides the densest subgraph (by sorting the vertices by $b_u$ and applying a standard peeling process in this order). However, when using gradient descent or FISTA, we only obtain an approximate solution ($\hat{b}, \hat{x}$) after $k$ iterations for the convex program. Fractional peeling, introduced in [1], is inspired by standard peeling but utilizes the **fractional values** of $b_u$ and $x_uv$ to determine a "good" ordering—hence the name fractional peeling. It "peels" the vertex $u$ with minimum $b_u$ fractional load value, then for each of its neighbors $v$, it subtracts the **fractional edge weight** $x_{uv}$ from $b_v$ (remember normal peeling would just subtract 1 signifying $u$ was deleted). > Any quick estimates of improvement using FISTA over Greedy++? What might be a good way to look at that question? Does it make sense to just use a random graph with, say, 1000 nodes and run the function? In C++, the main advantage of FISTA over Greedy++ is its parallelizability—you can use 4-10 threads, achieving orders-of-magnitude speedup since Greedy++ is inherently sequential. In Python, the difference is even more drastic. For example, in the benchmark "test_fista_big_dataset" on a graph with ~3,300 vertices and ~100,000 edges (in the tests), FISTA finds the densest subgraph in ~0.29 seconds, compared to ~15.89 seconds for Greedy++, yielding a **~55x speedup**: (I removed the Greedy++ test from the official tests since it takes too long). The primary reason is that FISTA allows for NumPy vectorization, making all operations nearly as fast as C, whereas Greedy++ does not benefit from such optimizations since unfortunately it is very sequential. That said, Greedy++ isn’t completely obsolete—it can converge quickly on some graph classes. As for what graphs to apply it on, all the graphs in the test suite right now are *really* hard and adversarially designed to challenge all these approximation algorithms. On a random graph, or even arbitrary "real world" graphs, these algorithms converge extremely fast, often taking for example 10-20 iterations at most which was shown in a lot of prior work practically. This was tested on graphs with even up to hundreds of millions of vertices and more than one hundred million edges.
